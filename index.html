intro

Investments don’t just generate alpha; they also anchor future beta (market trends) by consistently rewarding certain behaviors from managers of companies.

When investors blindly pour money into thematic ETFs, they think they are buying the future. In reality, they are usually subsidizing corporate hype cycles. Whether it's greenwashing, AI-washing, or supply-chain-washing, rewarding CEOs for hype rather than execution destroys long-term alpha and creates 'performative beta traps'.

These ‘performative beta traps’ create:

  • more uncertainty, since meaningful information is drowned out by investor-pleasing banalities;
  • more volatility, as markets thrive on announcements and marketing stunts;
  • and potentially lower future returns, as CEOs would prefer investing in performative short-term projects rather than foundational long-term ones.

In fact, most of today’s portfolio returns come from this market beta: Lukomnik and Burckart, 2026 identifies that 75-94% of actively managed and diversified institutional portfolios’ return variance comes from beta, dwarfing contributions from alphas.

In a backtest of our proposed form of thematic ETF, Intel for instance was kicked out in March 2023 and stayed out, even after becoming the largest CHIPS Act grant recipient (see figure 4 below for explanations).

By baking meritocratic filters into protocol-based funds, we aim for the market to collectively reward execution and make the criteria for receiving capital explicit — correcting for “performative” beta traps. What these ETFs sell is a competition process as well as a portfolio: a set of published rules that investors can inspect and companies can respond to. Such competition is at the basis of the meritocratic promise that free markets were supposed to deliver, and is here formalized into a protocol that compensates for our individual attention limits, lottery-like risk preferences, and bounded rationality.

Much of the difference between the US and European results comes from one US company, Bloom Energy. The US equal-weight control held it from the June 2019 start, and its share price rose about 25-fold between June 2024 and June 2026, but its accounting scores kept both strategies out of it until 30 September 2026. Over the long window, Bloom added 148 percentage points to the equal-weight return and accounts for 167 of the 321 points by which the US Q50 strategy trailed it (171 of 310 for Q75); no single European stock accounts for more than 73 points of either European gap. Part 1 - the Problem: recent behavioral economic highlighting inattention

In contrast to a fully rational investor who will perform due diligence with close attention before composing their portfolio, real life human beings are more sensitive to "shorthands", such as highly visible signs of success (call it charisma), or of competence (call it eloquence).

Research (Barber and Odean 2008, Da, Engelberg, and Gao 2011, van der Beck 2021, Barber, Huang, Odean, and Schwarz 2022) showed that both individual investors and institutional passive flows are increasingly net buyers of "attention-grabbing" stocks—stocks in the news, with extreme abnormal trading volume, or with extreme one-day returns. Because investors cannot systematically search thousands of stocks, they filter the universe down to what crosses their screens. A behavioral preference for lottery-like returns further compounds such distortions.

Mechanical piling up of investments (especially from indexed ETFs and retirement funds) then causes price bumps, but mostly temporary and reversing within a year (Da, Engelberg and Gao 2011) or even within a month (Barber, Huang, Odean and Schwarz 2022). These swings are especially marked for thematic ETFs (van der Beck 2021).

This is especially disheartening, as:

  • (1) these thematic ETFs were designed to encourage beneficial behaviors but now fall prey to performative traps, as CEOs are encouraged to XYZ-wash their companies to be included and benefit from the short-term price bumps;
  • (2) these thematic ETFs have been pushed by the industry to justify high management expense ratios (MERs), of often more than five times the fees for indexed and broad-based ETFs….
    ….but often perform much less well than the latter (underperformance of about 30% vs broad-based indices, as they compete on attention and therefore are often launched at the peak of a hype cycle - see Ben-David, Franzoni, Kim and Moussawi 2021, Morningstar’s Global Thematic Funds Landscape Report).

A missing governance layer: what markets can learn from inter-jurisdictional KPIs

If beta and expected returns are shaped by systemic distortions, then efforts to address those distortions—through shareholder voting, engagement, and policy advocacy—become part of the strategy for achieving higher returns (Lukomnik and Burckart, 2026).

There are already attempts by large asset managers to integrate system-level considerations into stewardship (Ambachtsheer and others, 2026 provides case studies from biodiversity to labor standards).

Scaling these sporadic initiatives into a coherent and efficient framework requires careful design. We propose automated portfolio rebalancing following new dimensions such as:

  • Eligibility: A documented subsidy or qualifying policy commitment brings a company into the candidate universe.
  • Scoring: Each quarter, companies are assessed on quantified measures such as subsidy use, returns on invested capital and project delivery, governance and political-risk metrics (see Section 3 below).
  • Exit: A holding is removed if its composite score falls into the bottom quartile of its candidate universe.
  • Re-entry: An excluded company remains under review. It returns only after scoring above the relevant bottom-quartile cutoff for two consecutive quarters.

Consider Micron in the US Q50 backtest. It is eligible from July 2019, when a Virginia state grant for its Manassas plant took effect; its CHIPS Act award followed in December 2024. Its score combines two measures ranked against eligible US companies: operating return on capital employed and cash earnings quality.

In the backtest, admission and re-entry instead require a score above the regional median at a single quarterly review. An existing holding exits when its score falls to or below the regional bottom-quartile threshold.

At the September 2023 exit, Micron’s measured operating return on capital employed had fallen from 39.9% at initial selection to −4.5%. Its weak profitability rank pulled the composite score below the exit threshold despite a strong cash-earnings-quality rank.

This type of design draws on a line of research on the roots of market efficiency, information sharing and coordinated planning, going back to von Mises, his student Hayek and others. This research could help craft the incentives and financial vehicles that would implement this missing corporate governance layer:

  • Hayek’s “knowledge problem” recognized the practical impossibility for a few institutional investors to steward an economy of continental scale and complexity.
  • So vast economic management authority should be devolved to agents who possess the relevant "local knowledge" that Hayek deemed essential for rational economic action.
  • There is however a trade-off between competition and coordination between these local agents, which can cause “market failures” or “externalities” via unpriced information, network dynamics (Arthur 1989, Haldane 2009, 2014), or captured institutions (Coase 1960, Ostrom 1990, Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, & Welch 1998).
  • There should therefore be new markets to correct for these externalities, including the performative beta trap. Carbon markets are an example, but following Lange-Lerner's intuition we argue that these new markets should not be built in isolation from existing financial markets, but rather as superimposed attention allocation mechanisms. Indeed, what’s the point of building separate carbon markets that no one will care for, if mainstream financial markets will still reward polluting companies based on profitability only?
  • An extreme example of an attention allocation mechanism is China’s inter-jurisdictional competition, in which local officials’ political career progression is evaluated against KPIs set by the central planning authorities in Beijing. Many researchers (Maskin, Qian and Xu 2000, Blanchard and Shleifer 2001, Li and Zhou JPE 2005, Xu 2011 etc) point to this design as one of the main factors explaining China’s economic development, despite massive waste of public money, corruption, and authoritarianism. Note that such inter-jurisdictional competition doesn’t require authoritarianism - any political party could implement it within a multi-party and democratic system. 
  • A second wave of Austrian critique (Hayek 1968, Kirzner 1973, Don Lavoie 1985) emphasized that the market is not primarily a mechanism for calculating prices; rather, it is a dynamic process for discovering new information, new technologies, new products, and new organizational forms.

Part 2 - The Solution: Organize competition and coordination via an attention market

In this framework, thematic ETFs are actually competing on investors’ attention. The problem lies in their hype-based portfolio selection, which tanks both their returns (when hype dies) and their purpose (as such selection encourages XYZ-washing).

The second wave of Austrian critique laid out instead a directive to turn competition into beneficial information diffusion, rather than harmful performative traps. The current subjective co-optation of firms into a thematic portfolio should be replaced by transparent, objective and uniform evaluations of whether a firm does belong.

The composition of an ETF could change, and its allocations be rebalanced - and this provides a feedback loop to continuously keep all firms competing for attention and in this theme on their toes.

What such ETFs sell to investors is therefore not a portfolio of firms related to a theme, but rather the competition itself between all firms seeking to joining an investment theme.

Below we set out the proposed framework and test the parts for which we have usable historical evidence. We provide the captured inputs, portfolio ledgers and code needed to reproduce the results at this github repository. In addition an open sourced codebase (see this github repository) allows readers to create their own algorithms that can be linked to brokers to keep running such portfolios in real or paper market conditions.

03

Scoring pillar 1: KPI scoring filtering performance

What it does. This pillar replaces the market’s subjective assessments — sentiment analysis, CEO charisma, earnings call tone… — with hard operational metrics. It is the direct analogue of the political inter-jurisdictional competition protocol’s KPI-based evaluation: a transparent scoring system evaluates every candidate on the same criteria and promotes best performers.

How it works. Every quarter, each company in the ETF universe receives a composite score across six metrics (here tailored for the government subsidy ETF case):

  • Subsidy utilization rate — measures what fraction of awarded government funds have been drawn down and deployed productively, at what speed is a company achieving the milestones defined by policymakers in the grant programs, and the CapEx execution rate.
  • A narrative discount factor — companies with high narrative salience but declining fundamentals are often overpriced (Bordalo, Gennaioli, and Shleifer). A factor can then be defined to measure the gap between analyst sentiment (earnings call NLP, ROIC spread, media mentions, analyst rating dispersion) and hard KPI trajectory. A company with rising sentiment but flat KPIs is a performative trap. One with flat sentiment but rising KPIs is an undervalued executor.
  • An institutional quality factor — the quality of governance structures often determines whether resources will be used productively (Williamson's transaction cost economics and North's institutional economics). Such an institutional quality factor would then score companies on governance proxies, such as capital allocation consistency over 5+ years, insider ownership alignment, board independence ratio, or an alignment score based on Jensen and Meckling's principal–agent problem.
  • A political economy risk —concentrated interests capture diffuse public benefits (Mancur Olson). Therefore, companies that lobby hardest for subsidies may be the worst at utilizing them, because their comparative advantage is in rent-seeking rather than production. A political economy pillar would measure lobbying intensity relative to operational scale; for instance high lobbying per dollar of revenue could be a negative signal.

The exit rule. Any company in the portfolio that falls into the bottom quartile of composite KPI scores is removed from the ETF. No exceptions, no overrides. This is the protocol’s teeth — it prevents the fund from holding a “story stock” that has stopped delivering.

The re-entry rule. Companies not part of the portfolio but still in this space are also scored every quarter. Any company that lifts its execution metrics out of the bottom quartile for two consecutive quarters is re-entered into the ETF.

An example: the Intel test. In the US Q50 backtest, Intel was removed from the ETF at the March 2023 review, after its operating return on capital employed fell from 13.8% to 1.6%, the fourth-lowest of the 14 scored US companies, pushing its score into the bottom quartile. It stayed out even after being awarded the largest CHIPS Act grant (up to $7.9B, finalized in November 2024). The protocol did what sparse-attention markets struggle to do: separate the size of the subsidy from the quality of the execution. This is the meritocratic filter in action.

04

Scoring pillar 2: Industrial Policy

What it does. This pillar identifies which companies have received confirmed government subsidies, grants, tax credits, or state aid — and uses these policy commitments as the entry ticket into the ETF universe.

The logic. When governments commit billions to specific sectors — semiconductors, clean energy, aerospace — they are making a long-duration bet on strategic importance. These bets create structural demand that outlasts business cycles. The CHIPS Act’s $52.7B doesn’t vanish when sentiment shifts; the IRA’s production tax credits run for a decade. France 2030’s €54B is allocated across five-year windows. The EU Chips Act has catalyzed over €80B in total investment. These are infrastructure commitments with contractual obligations.

How it works in the US ETF. The CHIPS basket (60% weight) holds companies with confirmed Commerce Department grants or loans: TSMC ($6.6B), Micron ($6.1B + $7.5B loans), GlobalFoundries ($1.5B + $1.6B loans), Texas Instruments ($1.6B), plus indirect beneficiaries like equipment makers whose order books depend on subsidy-funded fab construction. The IRA basket (40% weight) holds companies benefiting from manufacturing tax credits, hydrogen production credits, clean energy credits, and carbon capture credits.

Scoring pillar 3: Competition and Collaboration

What it does. This pillar measures where a company sits within the web of supply chain relationships created by industrial policy. It captures the insight that inter-jurisdictional competition creates diffusion effects — when one jurisdiction innovates, neighbors copy. In markets, the equivalent is supply chain contagion: when one company builds a fab, its equipment suppliers, materials providers, and downstream customers all benefit.

The concept: network centrality and spillover intensity. Each company is scored on how central and non-substitutable it is within a competitive-collaborative cluster. A company that supplies many other companies in the ETF universe, and has few substitutes, receives a high network centrality score. Companies whose patents are heavily cited by others are generating spillovers and should receive higher scores because policy shocks to them cascade more broadly — meaning subsidy dollars have higher multiplier effects (Acemoglu, Carvalho, Ozdaglar, and Tahbaz-Salehi, 2012, Romer 1990, Aghion and Howitt 1992) .

The ASML example. ASML Holding manufactures the only extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines in the world. Every advanced semiconductor fab requires ASML equipment. It would have the highest network centrality score in either ETF. This is the ultimate competition-collaboration position: ASML competes with no one in its niche, yet collaborates with everyone in the ecosystem.

The negative case. Nel ASA, a Norwegian hydrogen electrolyzer manufacturer, would score low on network centrality because the hydrogen value chain was not yet mature — there were too few downstream buyers and no established cluster. Despite being an IPCEI Hydrogen participant, Nel was operating in isolation. The pillar would flag that policy commitment alone, without network effects, is insufficient.

06

How the Three Scoring Pillars Interact

No single pillar drives the ETF in isolation:

  • Pillar 2 defines the universe — only companies with confirmed subsidies enter.
  • Pillar 1 filters within that universe — subsidy receipt is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Pillar 3 weights the survivors — among companies that pass the KPI filter, those with higher network centrality receive larger positions.
  • In the backtests, Pillar 2 admits each of the 31 candidates only from its first dated evidence of public support, which kept TSMC out until December 2024; the Pillar 1 score then drove every entry and exit.
